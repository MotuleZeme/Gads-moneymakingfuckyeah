How this calculator works

The calculator runs every input through a fixed set of steps, converting to metric internally so the mile/kilometer toggle never introduces rounding drift.

  1. Energy at the wheel. Monthly distance divided by your vehicle's efficiency gives the kWh actually used to move the car.
  2. Energy drawn from the wall. Home charging isn't 100% efficient, so the energy at the wheel is divided by (1 minus your charging loss percentage) to get what your meter actually measures.
  3. Monthly cost. Energy from the wall multiplied by your electricity rate.
  4. Gas comparison cost. Calculated the same way a fuel gauge would: miles divided by mpg, times price per gallon (or liters per 100 km, times price per liter).
  5. Savings. The gas cost minus the electric cost, shown monthly and annualized.

Tips for lowering your home charging costs

  • Charge on an off-peak rate. If your utility offers time-of-use pricing, overnight rates are often 30% to 50% cheaper than daytime rates. Most charging happens overnight anyway, so scheduling it to start after peak hours costs you nothing in convenience.
  • Level 2 beats Level 1 on efficiency, not just speed. A standard outlet (Level 1) is slower and typically has slightly higher conversion loss than a dedicated 240V Level 2 circuit. If you're charging daily, the upgrade usually pays for itself in both time and a lower cost per mile.
  • Charge to the range you need, not always to 100%. Topping off to 80% for daily driving instead of a full charge every night reduces the total kWh you pull each month and is generally easier on the battery long-term.
  • Watch for demand charges on high-amp setups. Some utilities, especially for larger homes or commercial-rate accounts, charge based on peak power draw, not just total energy. A high-amp Level 2 charger pulling 11+ kW at once can push your household into a higher demand tier. Check your rate plan before installing one.

How to read your comparison result

The comparison bars show your estimated monthly electric cost next to what the same driving would cost in a comparable gas car. A shorter electric bar means charging at home is cheaper per mile for the rate and efficiency you entered, which is true for the large majority of U.S. electricity rates and EVs.

The savings figures are a direct subtraction of gas cost minus electric cost. They don't include the price difference between the vehicles themselves, maintenance, insurance, or any purchase incentives, since those vary too much by situation to estimate generically. Treat this result as your ongoing fuel-cost comparison, not a full cost-of-ownership calculation.

If your result looks off, the two inputs worth double-checking first are charging loss (12% is typical, but older equipment can run higher) and electricity rate (use your actual per-kWh rate from a recent bill, not a national average).

Frequently asked questions

What's a typical EV efficiency range?

Most electric vehicles fall between roughly 2.5 and 4.5 miles per kWh (about 1.5 to 2.8 km per kWh), depending on the vehicle's size, weight, and driving conditions. Smaller EVs and sedans tend to sit at the efficient end; larger SUVs and trucks sit lower. Cold weather and highway speeds both reduce efficiency.

What charging loss percentage should I use?

Home charging typically loses 8% to 15% of the electricity drawn from the wall before it reaches the battery, mostly as heat during AC-to-DC conversion. 12% is a reasonable default for a Level 2 home charger. Level 1 setups and older equipment can run slightly higher.

Is installing a Level 2 charger worth it?

If you drive more than a short commute daily, yes. Level 1 (a standard outlet) adds only 3 to 5 miles of range per hour, which struggles to keep up with daily driving. Level 2 adds 20 to 40 miles per hour, fully replenishes overnight, and runs slightly more efficiently, which lowers your effective cost per mile.

Do time-of-use electricity rates actually help?

Often, yes. Many utilities offer overnight off-peak rates that run 30% to 50% below the standard rate. Since most home charging happens overnight anyway, shifting or scheduling charging to that window can meaningfully lower your monthly cost without changing anything else about your driving.

Does battery size affect my charging cost?

No. Your monthly cost depends on how far you drive and how efficiently your car uses electricity, not the size of the battery. Battery size only affects how long a full charge takes and how much range you have between charges.

How accurate is this calculator?

It's an estimate built from the inputs you provide. Your actual utility bill can differ based on tiered pricing, time-of-use rates, seasonal adjustments, and any fixed monthly charges your utility applies. Use it to compare scenarios, not as a substitute for your bill.
